Budget answers: **"What is happening to my money?"** Portfolio represents accumulated wealth at a point in time; Budget examines the movement of money over a period.

## Categorization

Transactions imported from connected accounts are automatically analyzed and assigned to a category. Categories are organized in two levels: sixteen **groups** — Housing, Daily living, Transport, Subscriptions, Health, Taxes and the rest — each holding the categories that belong to it, so Rent, Mortgage and Home insurance all sit under Housing. Income uses the same vocabulary, split into Salary, Self-employment, Investment income, Refunds and other sources.

Every group also carries a catch-all category — Other housing, Other transport — for the cases where a bank's data identifies the group but nothing narrower. Guessing a specific category from a signal that does not support one would read as precision that is not there.

What decides the category is read in order, and the order is deliberate: your own past correction for that merchant, then a known merchant, then the data the bank sends with the transaction — the merchant category code, the bank's own transaction code, the wording your country's banks use for rent, salary, tax or insurance. These give the same answer every time for the same transaction, and they settle the common case on their own. Only what they leave open reaches the classifier that reads the description, and only where a trained model is installed. When neither is confident, the transaction stays **Uncategorised** instead of being guessed into a category you would have to correct.

A **cash-flow diagram** draws the whole picture in one pass: income sources on the left, then the groups, then the categories inside each group. Clicking a group filters the transaction list to everything it holds; clicking a single category narrows to just that one. Income the period did not spend sits beside the groups as a grey **Money left** band — it holds no categories and is not clickable, since there are no transactions behind it.

Users can correct automatic classifications and create their own categories and groups. Custom categories are managed in [Settings](/docs/settings). The system learns from these corrections: repeatedly categorizing a merchant the same way makes that preference apply automatically in the future.

A **rules engine** complements this with predictable automation — e.g. "every transaction containing this merchant name is groceries" or "exclude this transfer from spending calculations". Rules handle what can be expressed statically; AI handles the rest.

## Budget periods

Budget cycles are configurable rather than assumed to run from the first to the last day of a calendar month. Users paid on the 25th can budget from the 25th to the 24th. Monthly, quarterly, yearly and arbitrary date ranges are supported.

## Transfers are not expenses

Cash-flow calculations distinguish real spending from movements between the user's own accounts. Moving €2,000 from a current account into a brokerage account is not a €2,000 expense; transferring between two bank accounts must not inflate both income and expenses. A normalized transaction model is essential for accurate budgets.

## Recurring expenses and subscriptions

Freenary analyzes historical transactions to detect repeating payments: streaming subscriptions, insurance premiums, rent, utilities, software services, loan payments and memberships.

- **List and calendar views** show when commitments occur during the month and reveal periods of unusually high cash requirements.
- **AI detection** catches recurring payments whose descriptions or amounts vary slightly.
- **Change monitoring** flags unexpected increases — e.g. a subscription rising from €12.99 to €15.99 three months ago, an extra €36 per year.
- Over time, Freenary could help _evaluate_ recurring expenses: unused services, expensive telecom plans or overlapping subscriptions. Such recommendations remain suggestions, never automatic actions.
